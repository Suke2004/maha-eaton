B. Project Deep-Dives (win the interview here) 8

Q8 "Walk me through your churn project, end to end." ▼

Always start from the business problem, not the code:

"The problem: a company is losing customers, and it's cheaper to keep an existing customer than to find a new one. So the question was — can we spot, from past data, which customers are about to leave?

Step 1 — Data: 5,630 customer records with 20 features — satisfaction score, distance traveled, cashback, how often they complain.

Step 2 — Cleaning: handled missing values, fixed data types, and used encoding to convert text categories — like city tier or gender — into numbers, because models only understand numbers.

Step 3 — Understanding the data: exploratory analysis — I checked which features were actually connected to churn. Complaints and satisfaction came out strong.

Step 4 — Modeling: I tried multiple classification models and compared them using cross-validation — I'll explain that next — and XGBoost gave the most stable and highest performance: 97.51% accuracy and 98.2% F1-score.

Step 5 — Deployment: I built a Flask app so a non-technical user can enter customer details and instantly get a churn-risk prediction.

What I learned: accuracy is not the goal — a useful model that someone can actually use is the goal. That's why I built the app instead of stopping at the notebook."

Timing: 2 minutes flat. If they want details, they'll ask — that's Q9–Q12 waiting.
Q9 "Why XGBoost? What is cross-validation?" ▼

"I didn't pick XGBoost because it sounds fancy — I let the numbers decide. I first tried simpler models like logistic regression and a decision tree, then compared them using cross-validation. In plain words: I split my data into 5 parts, trained on 4 and tested on 1, and repeated this 5 times so every part got a turn as test data. This gives an average score, so the result isn't just lucky on one split. XGBoost gave the best and most stable average, so I selected it."

Q10 "Accuracy vs F1-score? Why report F1?" ▼

"Accuracy is simply — out of all predictions, how many were right. But churn data is usually uneven — far more customers stay than leave. A model can say 'everyone will stay' and still look accurate.

F1-score balances two things: precision — of the customers I flagged as churners, how many really churned — and recall — of all the real churners, how many did I actually catch.

I reported both — 97.51% accuracy and 98.2% F1 — because in a real business, missing a real churner costs money."

Q11 "How did you handle missing values and categorical data?" ▼

"For missing values, I first checked why they were missing — sometimes it's random, sometimes it means something. Then I either filled them with a sensible value like the median for numbers, or dropped rows only when the missing piece was too much to guess.

For categorical data — like city or gender, which are words — models need numbers, so I used encoding: one-hot encoding for categories with no order, and label encoding where there's a natural order like a tier system. The rule I followed: never let information be lost, and never make up values that change the meaning of the data."

Q12 "What does the Flask part do? Why build it?" ▼

"The model lives inside a Python file — a normal user can't use that. Flask is a small Python tool that creates a simple web page. I connected the model to it, so a user can type in customer details — like satisfaction score and complaints — click predict, and get a risk answer like 'high chance of churn' in real time.

I built it because a model sitting in a notebook helps no one. Making it usable is what makes it data engineering — turning analysis into something people can actually work with."

Q13 "Explain your RAG chatbot — chunking, embeddings, FAISS?" ▼

"The problem: I wanted a chatbot that answers questions only from my own documents instead of making things up from the internet. Here's how, in simple steps:

  1. Chunking — I cut long documents into small pieces, a few hundred words each. A huge document is too much for the model at once; small pieces are easier to search. I processed over 500 chunks from 10+ documents.
  2. Embeddings — each chunk becomes a list of numbers that captures its meaning. Similar meanings get similar numbers — like giving every paragraph an address based on meaning.
  3. FAISS — a tool from Meta that stores these numbers and finds closest matches fast. When a user asks a question, I convert the question the same way and ask FAISS: 'which 3–4 chunks are most similar?'
  4. Generation — I send those chunks plus the question to the AI model, and it answers using only that context.

Result: responses stay grounded in my documents instead of the model guessing. Simple rule: give the model the right page, and it won't invent the story."

Draw this flow on paper 3 times today. Interviewers love candidates who can draw their system.
Q14 "What did you do at DRDL?" ▼

"I worked on signal-processing and digital waveform generation tasks. In plain words — I took signals, analyzed how they behave using MATLAB, built and tested designs on FPGA using Xilinx Vivado, and when simulation results didn't match what I expected, I dug into the discrepancy and fixed the design. I worked inside a structured defense environment where deadlines and accuracy both mattered, and I delivered my assigned tasks on time.

Q15 "What if your model's accuracy was only 70%?" ▼

"First, I wouldn't panic and start changing the model randomly. I'd ask: is 70% actually bad for this problem? If 70% of customers never churn, a lazy model gets 70% for free. So I'd check the F1-score and a confusion matrix first.

Then step by step: check data quality — maybe missing values or wrong encoding are hurting it; check if important features are missing; try feature engineering — combine existing columns into more useful ones; then try different models. And finally, I'd be honest with the team about what's achievable with this data. In my churn project, this stepwise check is exactly how I moved from basic models to 97.5%."

C. SQL + Python + Core Data Basics 18

Q16 SQL: second-highest salary ▼

"SQL is the language used to ask questions of a database — like Excel formulas, but for large tables."

SELECT MAX(salary) FROM employees
WHERE salary < (SELECT MAX(salary) FROM employees);

"Find the max, then the max of everything below that. If they ask for the second distinct highest with ties, I'd use DENSE_RANK()."

Q17 SQL: average salary per department ▼ 
SELECT department, AVG(salary) AS avg_salary
FROM employees
GROUP BY department;

"GROUP BY collects all rows of the same department into one group, and AVG calculates on each group."

Q18 SQL: count churned customers by city ▼ 
SELECT city, COUNT(*) AS churned_customers
FROM customers
WHERE churn = 1
GROUP BY city;
Q19 WHERE vs HAVING ▼

"WHERE filters rows before grouping. HAVING filters after grouping.

Example: WHERE salary > 10000 keeps only individual high-salary rows. HAVING COUNT(*) > 5 keeps only departments with more than 5 people — you can't use COUNT per group in WHERE, because WHERE runs before the groups are formed."

Q20 INNER JOIN vs LEFT JOIN ▼

"INNER JOIN gives only rows that match in both tables — common customers and common orders.

LEFT JOIN gives all rows from the left table, even with no match — every customer appears, and where there's no order, the order columns show NULL.

One-line memory: inner = intersection, left = everything from the left, plus matches."

Q21 SQL: delete duplicate rows, keep one ▼ 
DELETE FROM employees
WHERE id NOT IN (
  SELECT MAX(id) FROM employees
  GROUP BY name, email
);

"Logic: for each group of identical people, keep the one with the highest id, delete the rest."

Q22 Database vs Excel ▼

"Excel is like a paper register — great for one person, small data. A database is like a full library system — thousands of people can read and write at the same time, it enforces rules — like no duplicate account numbers — and it can store lakhs of rows without slowing down. Excel stores files; a database stores data with structure and safety."

Q23 What is ETL and a data pipeline? ▼

"ETL = Extract, Transform, Load.

Extract — pull raw data from different sources: a CRM export, an Excel file, an API.
Transform — clean it: remove duplicates, fix dates, fill missing values, join tables.
Load — put the clean data where reports and models can use it — like a data warehouse.

A pipeline is this whole journey set up to run automatically — like a water line from the tank to your tap. You turn it on, and clean data flows regularly without anyone manually copying files. Example: every night at 2 AM, yesterday's sales get pulled, cleaned, and loaded for the morning report."

This is the #1 definition to know — the role is literally "Data Engineering & Analytics".
Q24 Data engineer vs analyst vs scientist? ▼

"Data engineer builds the road — the pipelines that move clean data from source to destination.

Data analyst drives on that road and explains what happened — reports, charts, SQL queries, dashboards.

Data scientist builds the prediction — models like my churn project that say what will happen.

This role sits between engineering and analytics, which is exactly why I'm interested — I'd learn to build the pipelines and also do the analysis."

Q25 What is a data warehouse? ▼

"A big central store where cleaned data from many systems — sales, website, support — is brought together in one place, so reports and analysis are fast and consistent. Simple analogy: a kitchen's central fridge where all ingredients are prepped and labeled, instead of everyone running to different shops."

Q26 What is big data? ▼

"Big data means data that's too large or too fast for normal tools — think millions of transactions a day. People describe it with three V's: Volume (too big), Velocity (arriving too fast — live clicks), Variety (mixed types — logs, images, text). Frameworks like Spark handle it by splitting the work across many machines — like 10 friends dividing a huge assignment instead of one person doing it all."

Q27 What is data cleaning? Why does it matter? ▼

"Data cleaning is fixing a dataset before using it: removing duplicates, handling missing values, correcting wrong formats — like a date written as '05/03' meaning different things in different countries — and fixing impossible entries, like age = 200.

Why it matters: models and reports are only as good as the input — garbage in, garbage out. In my churn project, cleaning and encoding were a big part of the work, not a small step at the start."

Q28 What is an API? What is the cloud? ▼

"API is a waiter in a restaurant. You (one program) don't go into the kitchen (another program) — you give the waiter your order in a fixed format, and he brings back the dish. Apps use APIs to talk to each other — like a weather app calling a weather service.

Cloud is renting computers over the internet instead of buying and maintaining your own servers. Need 10× power for a festival week? Rent it, use it, switch it off. That's why companies use AWS or Azure — no big hardware bills."

Q29 Python: list vs tuple vs dictionary ▼

"List — changeable and ordered, like a shopping list you can edit: fruits = ['apple','banana'].

Tuple — fixed once created, like a date (2026, 9, 27) — you shouldn't change a date. Slightly faster, and usable as a dictionary key because it can't change.

Dictionary — key-value pairs, like a phone book: {'Mahalaxmi': 9876543210} — you look up by name, not position."

Q30 Python: remove duplicates while keeping order ▼ 
seen = set()
result = []
for x in my_list:
    if x not in seen:
        seen.add(x)
        result.append(x)

"Using a set makes lookup instant. If order didn't matter, list(set(my_list)) is quicker to write — but it shuffles the order."

Q31 Python: what is a function and why use one? ▼

"A function is a named block of code you can run again and again by calling its name — like a recipe. Benefits: no repeated code, easier to test and fix in one place, and the main logic stays readable. Example: def clean_data(df): — I write the cleaning once and use it on any dataset."

Q32 Pandas: DataFrame + handling nulls ▼

"A DataFrame is a table inside Python — like an Excel sheet with labeled rows and columns, but much faster and programmable."

df = pd.read_csv('customers.csv')
df.head()                                  # first 5 rows — always look before you trust

Q33 for loop vs list comprehension ▼

"Same job, different style. A for loop is explicit; a comprehension is the one-line version."

# for loop
squares = []
for x in range(5):
    squares.append(x*x)

# comprehension
squares = [x*x for x in range(5)]

"I use comprehensions for simple one-line jobs, normal loops when there's real logic — readability comes first."

Q34 What does try/except do? ▼

"It handles errors gracefully. If something can go wrong — user types text where a number is expected — you wrap it in try, and in except you decide what happens instead of the whole program crashing. Like a fuse box: when current spikes, the fuse cuts off instead of the house burning."

D. GenAI Basics (your advantage) 4

Q35 What is GenAI in simple words? ▼

"GenAI is AI that creates new content — text, images, code — instead of just classifying or predicting. It's usually built on a large language model, or LLM. An LLM is trained on huge amounts of text and learns to predict the next word given the words before it. Step by step, word by word, that's how it writes full answers. My chatbot project uses exactly this, plus retrieval so the answers come from my documents."

Q36 What is an embedding? ▼

"It's a way to turn text into numbers that capture meaning. Similar sentences get numbers that are close together — 'how do I reset my password' and 'forgot my login passcode' land near each other, even though they share almost no words. That's why search can work on meaning, not just keyword matching."

Q37 What is hallucination, and how does RAG reduce it? ▼

"Hallucination is when the model confidently states something that isn't true — it fills gaps with guesses. RAG reduces it by giving the model only relevant, real documents as context and asking it to answer from them. Not 100% removal — you should still check important answers — but it's the difference between an employee answering from the company manual versus from memory."

Q38 Where can GenAI be useful at PwC? ▼

"Many practical places: summarizing long audit or contract documents before a human reads them; drafting first versions of client emails or reports so the team only edits; answering internal questions from company knowledge bases — same idea as my chatbot; and cleaning or categorizing data. I'd start with low-risk, high-time-save tasks where a human always reviews the output."

E. Analytical / Consulting-Style 3

Q39 Case: "A client says their customers are leaving. What do you do?" ▼

"Four steps.

  1. Clarify — define 'leaving': cancellation? Inactivity for 30 days? And the time period.
  2. Understand the data — what do we have: transactions, support calls, complaints, app usage?
  3. Analyze — find when churn happens and what's common among churners: price change? complaints? competitor offers? Compare churners vs stayers feature by feature.
  4. Recommend — suggest what to do: complaints are the driver → fix support first; price → test a retention offer. And propose a simple model to flag at-risk customers weekly — exactly what my churn project does.

I'd always end with: what's the business action? Analysis without action is just numbers."

Q40 Guesstimate: ice creams sold in Durgapur on a summer Sunday ▼
